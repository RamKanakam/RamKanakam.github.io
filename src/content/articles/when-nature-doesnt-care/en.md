## Origins

A volcano bursts, spewing molten rock all around, causing death and displacement of people, animals, and destruction of houses and habitats. Sometimes the eruption may be so violent that it may cause an avalanche, sweeping everything in its wake. The aftermath is a great tragedy of loss and pain, for the lives of the living are abruptly altered almost irreversibly.

Watching their homes, which sheltered them in comfort from the harsh elements of nature, and which held the memories that define their lives, is a traumatic experience for anyone. If a loved one is lost in the event, it is even more of a scarring experience for life.

A star goes supernova, blasting everything around it and taking all life in its surroundings.

Neither the star nor the volcano intends to kill or damage anything. They are just following the natural laws. They have no agency. They do not will anything.

The death of people, animals, plants, or any other living beings around them does not cause them any pain. They do not weep for the tragedy they just unleashed, nor do they feel proud of the happy growth of life around them using the seeds of nutrients and energy supplied by them. In fact, depending on the climatic conditions, the ashes created by the volcano wither and create fertile soils, but the volcano does not care about it. Nor does the star care that the explosion it went into created heavier elements than iron and dispersed them in its vicinity, which are also essential for life.

As they destroy, they sow the seeds for new creation without any mercy. Both the volcano and the star do not feel either good or bad about it. They just do what they do, bound by the laws of nature.

And nature does not give a damn about our existence. Many call it mother nature, but it protects and nurtures us as much as it throws us into danger. Maybe this harshness is nature’s tough fatherly love. However, would a loving father’s tough love put us in mortal peril? Probably not.

So is nature good or bad? Nature does not want to nurture us, nor does it want to kill us. It does not want anything. It is what it is, and it is neither good nor bad. Its actions, not driven by any intention, are also neither good nor bad.

It is only us who put these happenings in the categories of good or bad. And how do we put it? We categorise them based on how the happenings or events are affecting the things we are attached to. Good and bad begin not in the event itself, but in the being to whom the event matters.

For example, a person halfway across the world, busy at work, probably goes, “Oh,” on hearing the news of the volcano and would continue with his work. To him, it’s just another event that doesn’t lessen the burden of the busy schedule ahead. Neither good nor bad.

A manufacturer of medical supplies might jump with enthusiasm at the opportunity unleashed by the volcano. To him, it’s good for business. A kid, who is the sole survivor from a family wiped out by the avalanche, might probably feel a pain he cannot express in words. To him, it’s a devastating experience, quite a bad one.

The attachment here is out of necessity or love that enriches our lives. The event is good if it positively contributes to our survival or happiness. So, a farmer who lost not only his livelihood but also essential food for his survival might feel the same as the kid. This is the same feeling that farmers across the world probably feel, being at the mercy of the forces of uncaring nature, which are still eluding our comprehensive understanding.

Let’s go back for a bit to a time when human civilization started to sprout. Agriculture or animal husbandry was essential for survival or to stay in power. Let’s consider the aspect of survival for now. Loss of crop at the time of harvest was not an uncommon thing even at that time. The cause for that could be rain, excessive or deficient.

Rain is a force of nature, like the volcano, that the farmer had no control over. Its effects are either a blessing or a punishment; a nail-biting uncertainty over whether he could survive and keep his loved ones or not.

To him, rain is powerful because it can inflict heavy loss on him in terms of harvest and his loved ones, who might starve to death or be swept off in a raging flood. Having had no control over rain, the uncertainty would have resulted in a lot of anxiety in him and his family. Losing the favour of rain meant certain doom.

To reduce this anxiety, he needs the amount of rain his farm demands. But what could he do to achieve that? While he had no understanding of the phenomenon behind rain, he might have gained the realisation that his human capabilities could not assert dominance over this uncertain force in order to reduce anxiety and improve his quality of life while it lasted.

But how does he ensure he gets the blessing of the rain? What actions does he need to take to reduce the anxiety? He might have tried many ways. Some of them might have seemed to work, but not with any certainty. For example, he might have pleaded for the rain to come during a time of deficit, but only a few lucky times it might have come when pleaded for.

So, rain listened to him only a few lucky times, like a petulant kid, a rather fate-alteringly powerful one. So what was the difference? Why did rain listen to him only sometimes and not at others? How did he get lucky during those times?

Luck is a kind of blessing that one needs to resolve uncertainty behind the screen and ensure favourable results. Again, how did he get lucky those few times? When rain was not going to answer this, his explanations probably were as good as anyone else’s. He probably would have pondered over this question, recollecting all that he did during those days, so that he could do the same again to get luck on his side and make rain listen to his pleas.

Whether he was lucky with rain listening or not, what’s wrong in trying it? At least he could rest assured that he tried all that he could: if rain listened, it’s good; if not, it wouldn’t hurt to plead. Luck might take his side this time, who knows?

Knowing that he did all he could might have put his mind at ease, free of any guilt, and might have improved the morale of his family and probably the group to which he belonged.

A person who got lucky many times might have been thought of as an expert in pleading with rain. Because of the positive effects of pleading, this recognition might have improved his position in the group, which usually resulted in comforts for him and his family. Anything can be addictive, and comforts, by their nature, are more so. This could have led to a desire for the position of a priest, although some of them might have taken the role in altruism, so as to keep the morale of the group up and running.

And this is probably how we got gods, prayers, rituals, and priests. Though rain is probably not the first god, the gods we know from early civilizations were all related to nature. And as such, they are not inherently good or bad. They are what they are, and we just categorise them at times depending on the effects they seem to have on the things we need or the things that bring us comfort.

The farming in the above case can be substituted with anything that one may need or want, so as to improve their chances of survival or just improve their comfort in this bubble of a life. After all, it’s not just surviving but surviving in comfort that has a ring to it, probably.

From the above, we can see that there’s no objective good and bad in nature. They are only good and bad in relation to how the events are affecting us or the people and things we care about.

So, there’s only good and bad, because we love.

The same can be said about hatred. One might feel relieved to know that their insufferably noisy neighbour lost the keys of his apartment. Hence, the origin of good and bad is with us. Nature doesn’t care about this distinction.

Thus, the wants and needs of a person determine what they consider to be good and bad for them. This determines their morality.

Then how does this connect to societal or cultural morality? Individual morality begins with what matters to a person; social morality begins when many people must live with what matters to each of them.

While what someone considers to be good or bad need not be the same as that of someone else, we as humans have many overlapping wants and needs. This leads to overlaps in what we care for, giving rise to what we consider as morality at the societal or cultural level.

What happens when the wants and needs of a person don’t match those of society? We need to understand why a society is important to a human to answer this.

So good and bad are not waiting inside nature. They arise in us, from what we fear losing, what we struggle to protect, what we hate, and what we cannot help loving.
